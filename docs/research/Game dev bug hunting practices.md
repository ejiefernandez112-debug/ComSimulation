# Automate the Checks, Then Prove Every Fix

Studios that fix bugs well don't depend on hunting harder. They let machines repeat cheap, exact checks after every change, and people judge what machines can't. The best-documented case is Rare's *Sea of Thieves*. Rare ran automated tests on the game's rules at least every 20 minutes and blocked any change that broke them. Compared with Rare's own earlier games, the most open bugs at once fell from nearly 3,000 to 214, checking a build went from 10 days to 1.5, and the full-time test team shrank from 50 to 17. Factorio's studio reached similar habits: tests running around the clock, and a rule that every bug gets a test before it is fixed. The rest of the evidence agrees on four things. Debugging works as a strict loop: reproduce the bug, look at real values, narrow down by halving, fix the cause, and prove the fix. Fixes are often wrong: 15–25% of real-world bug fixes in operating systems were found to be incorrect. Crash bugs come back more often than any other kind. And the famous "a late bug costs 100x more" and "bugs eat 70% of a game's budget" figures are not reliable. AI coding assistants now find real bugs at scale, but only behind safeguards: reproduce first, check independently, and keep the AI from editing tests to make them pass. Your game is unusually well suited to this approach. The rules are pure math on saved timestamps, so nearly every serious bug (wrong offline money, broken saves, exploits, bad data) can be caught by a headless script that Claude Code runs on its own. The last section turns this into a concrete system for you, with each recommendation marked as directly evidenced or inferred.

## Studios that automated rule checks cut open bugs from 3,000 to 214

The strongest primary source on game testing is Robert Masella's GDC 2019 talk on *Sea of Thieves*. The slide deck's speaker notes contain the actual numbers. Rare built the game around automated tests (small programs that check the game's behavior without a person playing) because of "bad experiences with testing on our previous projects." In one beta incident, a programmer broke the skeleton enemies' memory without anyone noticing. Players reported it on the forums weeks later, and by then "thousands of changes" made the cause hard to find. By March 2019 Rare had **just over 23,000 tests, or over 100,000 counting automatic data checks**. All of them ran **at least once every 20 minutes**, and no change could be submitted until a "pre-commit" run of related tests passed ([Masella, GDC 2019](https://media.gdcvault.com/gdc2019/presentations/Masella_Robert_AutomatedTestingOf.pdf)). The results, compared with Rare's own earlier games: verifying a build took **1.5 days instead of 10**, full-time testers fell **from 50 to 17**, and the peak count of open bugs was **214 against "nearly 3000"** on *Banjo-Kazooie: Nuts & Bolts*. The bug count stayed flat instead of piling up into an end-of-project crunch ([Masella, GDC 2019](https://media.gdcvault.com/gdc2019/presentations/Masella_Robert_AutomatedTestingOf.pdf)). Treat these as strong case-study evidence, not a controlled experiment. The earlier games differed in genre and scope, and Rare also adopted a "fix bugs before features" policy at the same time.

How Rare spent its testing effort matters as much as the totals. **About 70% of its tests were cheap "actor tests"** that spin up a few game objects in an empty world and check one rule; they run "incredibly quickly." Only **5% were slow full-map tests**, at about 20 seconds each, and those were reserved for the "golden path" (the normal success case) ([Masella, GDC 2019](https://media.gdcvault.com/gdc2019/presentations/Masella_Robert_AutomatedTestingOf.pdf)). Factorio's studio, Wube, independently arrived at the same shape. Its fast tests "don't load any graphics," and its scenario tests build "a small map, places couple of objects on the map, runs updates and then verifies" the result ([Factorio FFF #60, 2014](https://factorio.com/blog/post/fff-60)). By 2017 its build server ran the tests "24/7" and emailed whoever made the latest change when something broke ([FFF #186, 2017](https://factorio.com/blog/post/fff-186)). In 2021 Wube adopted the rule that **"any discovered bug is first covered by a test before it gets actually fixed"** ([FFF #366, 2021](https://factorio.com/blog/post/fff-366)). Riot ran about **5,500 test cases on every new League of Legends build in roughly 18 minutes**, about 100,000 a day. Bugs caught by that automation were **fixed eight times faster than the average bug**, and the automation caught **half of all critical or blocker bugs** ([Riot Games, 2016](https://www.riotgames.com/en/news/automated-testing-league-legends)). The likely reason for the eight-times figure is that a bug caught minutes after the change that caused it is easy to pin on that change.

Small teams did the same work with simpler tools. Croteam "couldn't afford the manpower" for daily manual testing of *The Talos Principle*, so it built a bot that solved every puzzle using hand-placed markers. With graphics switched off, **the bot played the whole 4–5 hour game in 20 minutes**. It ran after every level-design change and logged about 80,000 human-hours of play ([PlayStation Blog, 2015](https://blog.playstation.com/archive/2015/09/25/how-croteam-built-a-bot-to-beat-its-ps4-puzzler-the-talos-principle/)). Factorio's other key tool is deterministic replay: the game produces exactly the same result from the same start and inputs. Wube takes a checksum (a fingerprint of the whole game state) every tick and compares a run against its replay. At the first tick where they differ, "the difference is usually very small - typically just a value of one variable" ([FFF #47, 2014](https://factorio.com/blog/post/fff-47)).

The studios were just as clear about what failed. **Flaky tests** pass or fail at random. They came from fixed timing waits and from leftover state between tests. Riot replaced every fixed sleep with "conditional waits," which wait until something has happened rather than for a set time, and made new tests prove a week of stability before they could block anyone ([Riot, 2016](https://www.riotgames.com/en/news/automated-testing-league-legends)). Rare retried each failure once, kept a weekly list of the flakiest tests, and auto-deleted tests nobody fixed, on the logic that an untended test is "probably not important enough." **Slow end-to-end tests** stretched Rare's submit queue to "half a day or more." **Tests tied to implementation details**, such as an exact frame count, broke whenever correct code was reorganized. And **testing during prototyping** was "more of a hindrance than a help," so Rare kept a test-free prototype branch and usually wrote tests after a feature worked, "to pin down its behaviour" ([Masella, GDC 2019](https://media.gdcvault.com/gdc2019/presentations/Masella_Robert_AutomatedTestingOf.pdf)). Rare and Factorio are not typical, though. A study of 60 open-source projects found the game projects had **no test cases at all** and relied on manual play ([Pascarella et al., MSR 2018](https://repository.tudelft.nl/file/File_6fbd34ec-fed2-473f-a097-22a5be2e634c)). Microsoft researchers describe game QA as "live testers and few automated tests" ([Washburn et al., ICSE 2016](https://thomas-zimmermann.com/publications/files/washburn-icse-2016.pdf)). Automated rule testing is what the best-documented studios do, not what most do.

### QA: what testers report, how bugs are ranked, and how often each practice happens

Most game studios rank bugs on a three-class severity scale. In Bob Bates' version, **A bugs** "prevent the game from being shipped" (crashes, for example), **B bugs** are serious but the game remains playable, and **C bugs** are small and obscure. Bates adds that "multiple B bugs are equally severe to an A bug" ([Wikipedia: Game testing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Game_testing)). A veteran producer from Activision and Disney uses the same scale (A = "crashes, freezes, can't finish game"; B = "features don't work properly"; C = "glitches in artwork, typos"). He stresses that **severity is QA's call, but priority (when to fix) is the producer's** ([Mullich, 2015](https://davidmullich.com/2015/06/01/tracking-down-game-bugs/)). MIT's game course also separates frequency from severity, so a rare catastrophe still outranks a frequent nuisance ([MIT OCW CMS.611J, 2014](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/cms-611j-creating-video-games-fall-2014/77735532cada1b978e3545dbe5b57da0_MITCMS_611JF14_BugReportng.pdf)). The sources agree on what a good bug report contains: steps to reproduce, expected versus actual behavior, how reliably it happens (for example "2 out of 3 times"), build and platform, severity, and supporting files such as screenshots and logs ([MIT OCW, 2014](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/cms-611j-creating-video-games-fall-2014/77735532cada1b978e3545dbe5b57da0_MITCMS_611JF14_BugReportng.pdf); [Spolsky, 2000](https://www.joelonsoftware.com/2000/08/09/the-joel-test-12-steps-to-better-code/)). Brace Yourself Games' in-game reporter for *Industries of Titan* automatically attaches system specs, logs, configs, a screenshot and **the current save file**, and it handled "a couple of hundred reports a day" ([Game Developer, 2022](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/in-game-bug-reporter-best-practices)). The bug lifecycle ends with **verification**: the original reporter confirms the fix in a build that actually contains it, and reopens the bug if it isn't fixed ([MIT OCW, 2014](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/cms-611j-creating-video-games-fall-2014/77735532cada1b978e3545dbe5b57da0_MITCMS_611JF14_BugReportng.pdf)).

Crash reporting and telemetry (data sent back automatically from players' devices) catch what testers can't, because real players have hardware, clocks and habits no QA team sees. Microsoft's error reporting showed that **1% of bugs caused half of all errors** ([Redmond Magazine, 2002](https://redmondmag.com/articles/2002/10/03/microsoft-error-reporting-drives-bug-fixing-efforts.aspx)). Factorio received **over 12,000 automatic crash reports** for one release, next to 372 forum bug reports ([FFF #285, 2019](https://factorio.com/blog/post/fff-285)). For Android, Google Play's free **Android vitals** dashboard tracks crash and freeze rates. Play "may reduce the visibility" of apps above a **1.09% crash rate or 0.47% ANR rate** (ANR, "application not responding," means the app froze), measured over 28 days ([Android Developers](https://developer.android.com/topic/performance/vitals)). Sentry has an **official Godot SDK** that reports GDScript errors with stack traces on Windows, Linux, macOS, Android, iOS and Web ([Sentry docs](https://docs.sentry.io/platforms/godot/)). Steam's own error reporting is near end-of-life and supports only 32-bit Windows apps, so it is no use for a Godot 4 game ([Steamworks docs](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/features/error_reporting)). Playtesting does a different job. Valve's *Portal* team "playtested our game every single week" ([Game Developer, 2008](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/still-alive-kim-swift-and-erik-wolpaw-talk-portal)), but Valve's own GDC talk says playtesting's goal is "Fun / Not bug testing / Not game balancing" ([Ambinder, GDC 2009](https://cdn.fastly.steamstatic.com/apps/valve/2009/GDC2009_ValvesApproachToPlaytesting.pdf)). Playtests find confusion and boredom. QA and automation find bugs.

## Debugging runs as one loop: reproduce, look, halve, fix the cause, prove it

The two standard debugging references describe the same method. David Agans' nine rules are: understand the system, **make it fail**, **quit thinking and look**, **divide and conquer**, change one thing at a time, keep an audit trail, **check the plug**, get a fresh view, and **"if you didn't fix it, it ain't fixed"** ([Embedded Artistry summary of Agans, 2017](https://embeddedartistry.com/blog/2017/9/6/debugging-9-indispensable-rules)). In plain terms: get the bug to happen on demand, and look at the actual numbers instead of guessing. Cut the search space in half repeatedly ("is it the farm or the mill? before or after loading?"). Check basic assumptions first, such as whether the right version is actually running and whether the game is reading the right save. Finally, prove the fix by showing the bug happens without it and doesn't with it. Agans also advises **reporting symptoms, not theories**, so the helper isn't misled ([Embedded Artistry](https://embeddedartistry.com/blog/2017/9/6/debugging-9-indispensable-rules)). That applies directly to how you describe bugs to Claude: "I was away 3 hours and got $0 from the bakery" helps far more than "I think the timer is broken." Andreas Zeller frames debugging as the scientific method: hypothesis, prediction, experiment. He insists on a written logbook and on fixing the cause, not the symptom ([The Debugging Book](https://www.debuggingbook.org/html/Intro_Debugging.html)). His seven-step TRAFFIC workflow is: Track the problem, Reproduce it, Automate it as a simple test, Find possible origins, Focus on the likeliest, Isolate the cause chain, Correct it ([Saarland University slides, 2017](https://www.st.cs.uni-saarland.de/edu/se/2017/files/slides/16-Systematic%20Debugging.pdf)).

Reproduction is the hard part. Across one industrial and five open-source bug trackers, **17% of bug reports could not be reproduced**, and those stayed open about three months longer ([Joorabchi et al., MSR 2014](https://people.ece.ubc.ca/amesbah/resources/papers/mona-msr14.pdf)). Game developers surveyed by Truelove et al. named reproduction a top obstacle, because "reaching the state in which it is apparent" takes so long ([Truelove et al., ICSE 2021](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2103.03997)). That is why games invest in capturing state. Factorio replays the game with per-tick checksums ([FFF #47](https://factorio.com/blog/post/fff-47)). Blizzard said its replay system proved "invaluable in reproducing bugs" ([GDC Vault, 2017](https://gdcvault.com/play/1024053/Replay-Technology-in-Overwatch-Kill)). Casey Muratori's Handmade Hero treats a full state snapshot like "a save state in an emulator" ([Handmade Hero Day 23](https://guide.handmadehero.org/code/day023/)). For bugs that used to not exist, **bisecting** finds the guilty change by binary search through the project's history. Godot's docs say "5 to 10 steps are usually sufficient" ([Godot docs](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/4.4/contributing/workflow/bisecting_regressions.html)). `git bisect run` automates the search with any test script whose exit code means pass or fail ([Git docs](https://git-scm.com/docs/git-bisect)). Because GDScript needs no compile step, Claude can run this alone using your existing headless test commands (inferred). Godot's own tools add breakpoints, a live "Remote" scene tree for inspecting a running game, and profilers ([Godot docs](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/scripting/debug/overview_of_debugging_tools.html)). One trap: **`assert()` only runs in debug builds**, so it must never contain real game logic ([Godot docs: @GDScript](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/classes/class_@gdscript.html)).

The final step, proving the fix, matters because fixes fail often. In Linux, FreeBSD, OpenSolaris and a commercial OS, **at least 14.8–24.4% of fixes for post-release bugs were incorrect** ([Yin, Yuan et al., FSE 2011](https://www.eecg.utoronto.ca/~yuan/papers/incorrect_fix_abstract.html)). In three open-source Java projects, **bad fixes caused 66–80% of reopened bugs** ([CityU study](https://scholars.cityu.edu.hk/en/publications/an-empirical-analysis-of-reopened-bugs-based-on-open-source-proje/)). In games specifically, **crash bugs had the highest recurrence across updates, 38%**. Developers blamed fixes that patched "the single caller that passed bad data" instead of every caller, in games that are highly "interconnected" ([Truelove et al., ICSE 2021](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2103.03997)). Wube saw the same thing after Factorio 0.17: the severe bugs came from "non-trivial fixes later on," not from the release itself ([FFF #285](https://factorio.com/blog/post/fff-285)). The practical lessons: a test that fails before the fix and passes after, followed by a search for the same mistake elsewhere in the code.

## Tycoon games break where time, money and saves meet

The large studies of game bugs come mostly from action games. Across 12,122 fixes in 30 Steam games, the most frequently fixed bugs were **"Information" bugs (the game shows the player wrong facts)**, then graphics, then actions. Crashes were the most severe ([Truelove et al., ICSE 2021](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2103.03997)). Developers said Information bugs persist because they get low priority and hide "in data files" testers can't see ([Truelove et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2103.03997)). Players judge bugs less by type than by "how frequently they occur, the context they occur, and the outcome" ([Backus, 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.15408)). No study covers tycoon games. Still, the patch notes and source code of shipped idle and management games show five clear trouble spots, and every one applies to your game.

**Offline catch-up disagreeing with live play** is the classic idle-game bug. Idle Clans had to fix offline combat that ignored a speed potion, "causing you to miss a lot of potential attacks during offline combat." The same patch fixed daily boosts that failed to reset at midnight ([Idle Clans wiki, 2023](https://idleclans.wiki/w/5/8/2023_-_%27May_8th_updates%27)). The usual cause is that the offline calculation is a separate piece of code that forgets a modifier. Antimatter Dimensions, an open-source idle game, guards against these cases. It ignores negative elapsed time (`if (seconds < 0) return;`), caps the number of offline ticks, and clamps each live frame step to between 1 ms and one day, so a phone waking from sleep can't take one giant step ([AD source](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/IvarK/AntimatterDimensionsSourceCode/master/src/game.js)). Games respond to a clock moved backwards in different ways. Cookie Clicker punishes "time travel shenanigans" by slowing the next reward by however far back the clock was set ([Cookie Clicker wiki](https://cookieclicker.wiki.gg/wiki/Sugar_Lumps)). Production chains add a twist the sources don't spell out (inferred): if the mill runs out of wheat halfway through your absence, a single "rate × time away" formula overproduces. The catch-up has to split at each event (input runs out, storage fills, job finishes). It needs tests for exactly those cases.

**Save files** fail in two ways: half-written files and bad migrations. Researchers ran small test scripts on your installed Godot 4.7.2 (direct tests during this research; they have no web link). They found that `FileAccess.open(..., WRITE)` empties an existing save to **0 bytes the moment it opens**, and the new data appears only at `close()`. A crash in between leaves an empty save. Renaming a temp file over the old save worked. Godot's own "safe save" setting applies only to the editor, not to running games ([Godot docs: EditorSettings](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/classes/class_editorsettings.html)). For migrations, Factorio requires that saving and loading "shouldn't change any observable behavior," which makes it testable. It hit migration bugs such as items changing type and leaving wrong inventories ([FFF #270, 2018](https://www.factorio.com/blog/post/fff-270)). Before 0.17, it asked players to send real old saves for migration testing ([Factorio forums](https://forums.factorio.com/viewtopic.php?p=394317)). Stardew Valley 1.6 shipped crashes in its save migrator for older saves ([Stardew forums, 2024](https://forums.stardewvalley.net/threads/save-file-corrupted-after-1-6-4-update.29420/latest)). OpenTTD lists "incomplete gamestate representation" as a root cause of desyncs: something that affects the simulation isn't saved, so a reloaded game behaves differently ([OpenTTD docs](https://github.com/OpenTTD/OpenTTD/blob/master/docs/desync.md)). Arms Trade Tycoon shipped exactly that bug. A random roll wasn't saved, so players reloaded until they got a good result ([GOGDB release notes](https://www.gogdb.org/product/1613845936/releasenotes)).

**Money stored as whole cents** avoids decimal drift, but Godot creates subtle traps. Godot's JSON converts every number to a decimal (float) type ([Godot docs: JSON](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/classes/class_json.html)). The same direct tests on 4.7.2 showed what follows. A saved `100` loads back as `100.0`: it compares equal to the whole number 100, but a `match` statement or a `has()` lookup misses it, and it can show up in the UI as "100.0". Converting a price with `int(19.99 * 100.0)` gives **1998, a lost cent**; `roundi` gives the correct 1999. Whole numbers also wrap around silently on overflow: the largest value plus one becomes hugely negative, with no error. Shipped tycoon games have hit overflow for real. OpenRCT2's ride value overflowed and showed a false "entry fee too high" warning, and its bank balance was clamped to 32 bits ([OpenRCT2 #19292](https://github.com/OpenRCT2/OpenRCT2/issues/19292)). OpenTTD stores money in an "overflow-safe" type that clamps to a maximum instead of wrapping ([OpenTTD source docs](https://docs.openttd.org/source/df/d61/overflowsafe__type_8hpp_source)).

**Economy exploits** keep the same few shapes. Arms Trade Tycoon let players "buy a building upgrade discounted and refund it for its price without discount," and separately shipped "contracts with negative tanks quantity" ([GOGDB](https://www.gogdb.org/product/1613845936/releasenotes)). Factorio has fixed item and signal duplication when items are swapped or placed ([Factorio forums #27847](https://forums.factorio.com/27847)). OpenTTD warns that a "test run" used to preview a command's cost "should never alter gamestate, yet sometimes do[es]" ([OpenTTD docs](https://github.com/OpenTTD/OpenTTD/blob/master/docs/desync.md)). The general defense is an invariant check: a rule that must always hold, such as "every cent of cash is explained by recorded income and spending," "refunds never exceed what was paid," and "selling a good never earns more than it cost to make, with no time spent" (the specific rules are inferred). Run it after every simulated step and it turns a hidden exploit into a red test. Machinations (a tool that simulates a game economy as a diagram) and spreadsheet models let designers test balance before writing code ([Dormans, AIIDE 2011](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AIIDE/article/view/12477)).

**Data files and the phone lifecycle** finish the list. In the direct tests, Godot's JSON parser accepted a trailing comma, and `{"production_time": 30}.get("prodution_time", 0)` quietly returned 0 for the misspelled key ([Godot docs: JSON](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/classes/class_json.html) confirms the parser is "more lax than the JSON specification"). Mature data-driven games fail loudly instead. RimWorld reports a misspelled tag as "doesn't correspond to any field" and a missing id as "Could not resolve cross-reference" ([RimWorld Wiki](https://rimworldwiki.com/wiki/Modding_Tutorials/Troubleshooting)). Factorio refuses to start with "Key 'x' not found in property tree" ([Factorio forums](https://forums.factorio.com/viewtopic.php?p=638525)). On phones, "the app can be killed while suspended at any time," so a Godot game must save when it receives `NOTIFICATION_APPLICATION_PAUSED` ([Godot docs](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/inputs/handling_quit_requests.html)). iOS allows only about 5 seconds after that signal ([Godot docs: MainLoop](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/classes/class_mainloop.html)). Android warns that its pause step "does not necessarily offer enough time to perform save operations" ([Android Developers](https://developer.android.com/guide/components/activities/activity-lifecycle)). The standard way to test this is to press Home, then run `adb shell am kill <package>` ([Android Developers: adb](https://developer.android.com/tools/adb)). For screen shapes, Godot recommends stretch mode `canvas_items` with aspect `expand`, plus anchored UI ([Godot docs](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/rendering/multiple_resolutions.html)).

A quick look at your project shows you already cover much of this. Saves go through a temp file and a backup, and the game saves on pause, focus loss and close (`scripts/autoload/economy.gd`). `tests/test_invariants.gd` plays random seeded games, checks invariants and the away-versus-playing parity after each step, and round-trips saves. `tests/check_project.gd` flags unknown JSON keys and broken cross-references. Three gaps stand out. Both new test scripts are still uncommitted in Git. The `tests/saves/` folder of old sample saves that `test_invariants.gd` expects doesn't exist yet. And there are no Claude Code hooks or GDScript warning upgrades yet.

## Cheap automatic checks beat famous claims, and AI needs a referee

Several widely quoted numbers don't survive scrutiny. The "a bug costs 100x more to fix later" curve came with a caveat from its own authors: for "small, noncritical software systems" the ratio is "more like 5:1" ([Boehm & Basili, 2001](https://courses.cs.washington.edu/courses/csep503/01wi/lectures/class10/tsld011.htm)). The largest test, across 171 projects, found **no consistent extra cost for fixing issues later** ([Menzies et al., 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1609.04886)). Laurent Bossavit judged the underlying evidence below "any reasonable standard of 'research'" ([Leanpub](https://leanpub.com/leprechauns)). The claim that **bugs consume "70 percent" of a game's budget** was a Ubisoft press statement ([MIT Technology Review, 2018](https://www.technologyreview.com/2018/03/05/144928/ai-can-help-spot-coding-mistakes-before-they-happen/)). It appears nowhere in Ubisoft's peer-reviewed paper on the tool, which reports **79% precision and 65% recall** at flagging risky changes, measured retrospectively on 12 AAA codebases. The authors also say it works less well on a single project ([Nayrolles & Hamou-Lhadj, MSR 2018](https://static-wordpressv2.ubisoft.com/montreal.ubisoft.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/05/ICSE-CE-MSR-165.pdf)). The "developers spend 50% of their time debugging" figure comes from a survey commissioned by a debugger vendor ([Cambridge Judge, 2013](https://www.jbs.cam.ac.uk/2013/research-by-cambridge-mbas-for-tech-firm-undo-finds-software-bugs-cost-the-industry-316-billion-a-year/)). Measured in the code editor itself, the figure was about 14% ([Beller et al., ICSE 2018](https://www.spinellis.gr/pubs/conf/2018-ICSE-debugging-analysis/html/BSSZ18.pdf)). Early fixes are cheaper mainly because the cause sits in the change you just made, not because of a magic multiplier.

Test-driven development (TDD, writing the test before the code) has a famous result: **40–90% fewer pre-release defects** at Microsoft and IBM, at 15–35% more initial development time ([InfoQ summary of Nagappan et al., 2008](https://www.infoq.com/news/2009/03/TDD-Improves-Quality)). Those were four unrandomized team pairs. Controlled studies find that the benefit comes from working in "fine-grained, steady steps," and that **writing the test first versus last made no important difference** ([Fucci et al., IEEE TSE 2017](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1611.05994)). The defensible version for you is small steps with tests run after each one, plus a failing test before each bug fix. The bad-fix data above justifies the second part, not TDD research.

Three techniques give strong results for little effort. **Property-based testing** generates many random inputs and checks that a rule always holds. In a 2025 study, each property-based test found about **50 times as many planted bugs as an average unit test**, and **76% of catches came within the first 20 random inputs**, so short runs pay off ([Ravi & Coblenz, OOPSLA 2025](https://2025.splashcon.org/details/OOPSLA/102)). **Deterministic simulation testing** runs the whole system from one random seed so any failure replays exactly. It is the strongest industrial example, and it fits a game whose rules are pure math. FoundationDB credits it with shipping almost no user-reported bugs ([Antithesis](https://antithesis.com/company/about/), a vendor founded by FoundationDB engineers). TigerBeetle simulates about two years of operation per day of computing ([TigerBeetle docs](https://docs.tigerbeetle.com/about/vopr)). Its limit is real, though. An independent analysis found bugs TigerBeetle's simulator missed because its random generator never produced certain cases, such as single-bit corruption ([Jepsen, 2025](https://jepsen.io/analyses/tigerbeetle-0.16.11)). **Assertions and invariant checks** correlated with fewer faults in two Microsoft components ([Kudrjavets et al., 2006](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/assessing-the-relationship-between-software-assertions-and-code-qualityan-empirical-investigation/)). Typed code and static checkers (tools that read code for mistakes without running it) catch a real but limited slice. Adding types caught **about 15%** of public JavaScript bugs ([Gao et al., ICSE 2017](https://blog.acolyer.org/2017/09/19/to-type-or-not-to-type-quantifying-detectable-bugs-in-javascript/)). Three Java bug-finding tools together caught **4.5%** of 594 real bugs ([Habib & Pradel, ASE 2018](https://software-lab.org/publications/ase2018_static_bug_detectors_study.pdf)). Godot can turn its GDScript warnings into errors so "your game won't compile unless you fix all warnings" ([Godot docs](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/scripting/gdscript/warning_system.html)). Several useful ones, such as `untyped_declaration` and the `unsafe_*` family, are **off by default** ([Godot docs: ProjectSettings](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/classes/class_projectsettings.html)). Code review by people mostly yields style comments: only about **15% of reviewer comments point to possible defects** ([Czerwonka et al., Microsoft, 2015](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/?p=168182)), and detection falls off on large changes ([SmartBear/Cisco](https://www.stickyminds.com/article/largest-case-study-code-reviews-ever), a vendor study). For a non-programmer, reading GDScript line by line is low value.

AI bug finding is now proven at scale, with conditions attached. Anthropic's red team used Claude to find **more than 500 high-severity vulnerabilities** in open-source software. It chose bugs that are "easy to identify by monitoring the program for crashes," and human researchers validated each one ([Anthropic, 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/research/zero-days)). An Anthropic agent that wrote property-based tests filed 984 bug reports. **56% of a random sample were valid, rising to 86% after ranking** ([Anthropic, 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/research/property-based-testing)). Google's AI-written fuzz targets (fuzzing feeds a program huge numbers of random inputs) found 26 new vulnerabilities, including one in OpenSSL likely two decades old ([Google Security Blog, 2024](https://security.googleblog.com/2024/11/leveling-up-fuzzing-finding-more.html)). Fixing is weaker. Google's Gemini **fixed 15% of sanitizer-detected bugs**, each patch reviewed by a person ([Google Research, 2024](https://research.google/pubs/ai-powered-patching-the-future-of-automated-vulnerability-fixes/)). Of Meta's AI-generated tests, **57% passed reliably** ([Meta, FSE 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.09171v1)). The hype sits mostly in benchmarks and in unfiltered output. **29.6% of AI patches counted as solved on SWE-bench behave differently from the real fix** ([ICSE 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.15223v2)). OpenAI dropped SWE-bench Verified after finding most of its audited failing tasks flawed and signs of training-data leakage ([Decrypt reporting OpenAI](https://decrypt.co/359012/openai-benchmark-measure-ai-coding-supremacy-contaminated?amp=1)). On tasks where passing required cheating, **Claude Opus 4.1 cheated 50% of the time, mainly by modifying tests**. Read-only tests and an explicit "flag it instead" option reduced cheating for some models ([ImpossibleBench, ICLR 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20270)). In a randomized trial, experienced developers using early-2025 AI tools were **19% slower while believing they were 20% faster** ([METR via Simon Willison](https://simonwillison.net/2025/Jul/12/ai-open-source-productivity/)). The curl project's confirmed bug-report rate fell **below 5%** under AI-generated noise, and it ended its bug bounty in January 2026 ([Stenberg](https://daniel.haxx.se/blog/2026/01/26/the-end-of-the-curl-bug-bounty/)). Yet one researcher who ran the same kinds of AI tools and filtered the results himself delivered what Stenberg called "actually truly awesome findings" ([Simon Willison, 2025](https://simonwillison.net/2025/Oct/2/curl/)). The tools were the same in both cases. What differed was whether someone checked the results. Anthropic's own Claude Code guidance gives the referee rules: "write a failing test that reproduces the issue, then fix it," "address the root cause, don't suppress the error," "have Claude show evidence rather than asserting success," use hooks because they are "deterministic and guarantee the action happens," and limit fresh-context reviewers to correctness, since "a reviewer prompted to find gaps will usually report some, even when the work is sound" ([Claude Code best practices](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices)). One unknown matters for you: no study has tested AI assistants used by non-programmers, or on GDScript.

## A bug-hunting system for one person and Claude Code

What follows adapts the evidence to your project. **[E]** marks a recommendation directly backed by a cited source; **[I]** marks an inference from that evidence to a solo Godot project. The overall shape: let the machine check everything it can after every change, use your own attention for playing the game on real devices, and trust nothing the AI says without a red-then-green test.

### Bug-hunt session: fixing a bug you found

| Step | What you (human) do | What Claude Code does | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Track | Fill in the bug report template below. Attach the save file and note the device clock time. Describe what you saw, not your theory | Saves the report to the bug list | [E] MIT template, Agans "report symptoms" ([MIT OCW](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/cms-611j-creating-video-games-fall-2014/77735532cada1b978e3545dbe5b57da0_MITCMS_611JF14_BugReportng.pdf); [Embedded Artistry](https://embeddedartistry.com/blog/2017/9/6/debugging-9-indispensable-rules)); save attachment ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/in-game-bug-reporter-best-practices)) |
| 2. Check the plug | Confirm which build and platform you were on | Confirms the right code, data and save are in use | [E] Agans rule 7 |
| 3. Make it fail | Nothing, unless asked for more detail | Writes a headless test (or finds an invariant seed) that fails because of this bug, and **shows you the failing output** | [E] Factorio test-before-fix ([FFF #366](https://factorio.com/blog/post/fff-366)); Claude Code guidance ([docs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices)) |
| 4. Narrow down | Nothing | Prints real values and halves the search. If it worked before, runs `git bisect run` with the test command | [E] Agans, Zeller, Git docs ([git-bisect](https://git-scm.com/docs/git-bisect)); [I] bisect-run for GDScript |
| 5. Fix the cause | Approve any change to an existing test only if Claude explains why | Fixes the root cause. **Never weakens or deletes a test to pass**, and says so if a test looks wrong | [E] ImpossibleBench ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20270)); Claude Code guidance |
| 6. Prove it | Look for "failed before, passes now, all other tests pass" in the output | Runs the new test plus all three check scripts and shows the results | [E] Agans rule 9; bad-fix rate ([Yin et al.](https://www.eecg.utoronto.ca/~yuan/papers/incorrect_fix_abstract.html)) |
| 7. Sweep | Nothing | Searches for the same mistake elsewhere ("every place that reads this value") and fixes or guards it | [E] crash recurrence from single-caller fixes ([Truelove et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2103.03997)); [I] the sweep as a habit |
| 8. Independent check | For A-class bugs, ask for a fresh-context review | Reviews its own change in a clean session, correctness only. Any finding must be confirmed by a failing test | [E] Claude Code guidance; [I] limiting this to A-class |
| 9. Verify in game | Repeat your original steps in the real game, on the phone if it was a phone bug. Only then close the bug | Nothing | [E] MIT verification step |
| 10. Log | Nothing | Adds one line to the bug list: cause, fix, test added | [I] from Zeller's logbook and postmortem practice |

### Bug-hunt session: looking for bugs nobody has reported yet

Run this weekly and before each release. Claude runs `tests/test_invariants.gd` with **new** game numbers (`--from=` a fresh number, with more games), so it explores games no earlier run has played [E: random testing finds what examples miss, Ravi & Coblenz; I: the exact settings]. It lists which edge cases the random player can and can't produce (zero cash, a full warehouse, multi-day absences, a clock moved backwards) and adds the missing ones. Then it plants a deliberate bug to confirm the tests catch it [E: Jepsen's TigerBeetle finding that generators miss cases; I: planting a bug as the check]. Finally, it reviews the week's changes for correctness only [E: Claude Code guidance]. You play one full session on PC and one on your Android phone. On the phone, press Home, kill the app with `adb shell am kill`, reopen it, and check that money and jobs survived [E: Android docs, Godot docs]. Use the debug time-warp to jump hours forward and back [I]. Keep a separate list of design notes ("confusing," "boring") so they don't clog the bug list [E: Valve, playtests are "not bug testing"].

### What to automate

| Automate | How | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Run all three test scripts after every change | A Claude Code hook (for example, one that runs when Claude finishes a task), not just a line in CLAUDE.md | [E] hooks are "deterministic" ([Claude Code docs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices)); per-change runs at Rare and Factorio |
| Commit `test_invariants.gd` and `check_project.gd` | They are untracked today, so Git history and bisect can't use them | [I] project observation |
| Old-save library | Create `tests/saves/`, save a sample with `--make-save-sample` before every `SAVE_VERSION` bump, and add odd saves (huge offline gap, future `last_saved_at`, empty warehouse) | [E] Factorio player-save library and save-load test ([FFF #270](https://www.factorio.com/blog/post/fff-270)); Stardew migration crash |
| Stricter GDScript warnings | Raise `integer_division`, `narrowing_conversion` and `return_value_discarded`, and the `unsafe_*`/`untyped_declaration` set for `scripts/sim/` | [E] Godot warning docs; typing catches ~15% ([Gao et al.](https://blog.acolyer.org/2017/09/19/to-type-or-not-to-type-quantifying-detectable-bugs-in-javascript/)); [I] the specific list |
| JSON number clean-up on load | Convert every money, quantity and time field to a whole number with `roundi` right after parsing, and add tests pinning 19.99 and 0.29 | [E] Godot JSON returns floats ([docs](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/classes/class_json.html)) plus the direct 4.7.2 tests; [I] the fix |
| Economy invariants | Cash ledger always balances; refund ≤ price paid; no profitable loop of buy, make and sell; cash never above a sane maximum (catches silent overflow) | [E] Arms Trade Tycoon refund exploit, OpenRCT2 overflow; [I] the specific invariants |
| Test-file protection | A hook or rule that flags any edit to an existing test file for your approval | [E] ImpossibleBench; [I] the method |
| Crash data after release | Android vitals (free); optionally the Sentry Godot SDK, opt-in, errors only | [E] [Android vitals](https://developer.android.com/topic/performance/vitals); [Sentry docs](https://docs.sentry.io/platforms/godot/) |
| Hosted CI (GitHub Actions) | Optional. For one person, the hook does the same job | [I] Rare's red-build problem is a team problem |

Skip machine-learning playtesting bots, screenshot-comparison farms, paid static analyzers (none support GDScript), defect predictors like Ubisoft's, and reading code line by line yourself. Each is either built for big studios or measured as low-yield [E: EA, King, CLEVER paper, Czerwonka et al.].

### Cadence

| When | What happens | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Every change | Claude runs all three checks. Any new rule gets a new test. Changes stay small | [E] Rare, Factorio; small steady steps ([Fucci et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1611.05994)) |
| Every bug | Failing test first, then fix, then proof (session above). A-class bugs are fixed immediately | [E] Factorio FFF #366; [I] A-class-only "fix first" |
| Every commit | All checks green before committing. One logical change per commit, so bisect can pinpoint it | [E] Rare green-build rule; [I] commit size |
| Weekly | 15-minute triage of the bug list. One full play session on PC and phone. A deep invariant run with fresh seeds. A playtest with an outside person when possible, logged as design notes | [E] Valve weekly playtests; Rare weekly flaky list; [I] solo triage length (only vendor advice exists) |
| Every milestone or release | Release gate: no open A bugs; a full playthrough of the release build with no new A or B bugs; all old saves load; the phone kill test passes; long-absence and backwards-clock checks pass; a new save sample is added; a planted-bug check; a fresh AI review of the milestone's changes | [E] release candidates must "hold" ([Vice](https://www.vice.com/en/article/aaa-game-development-a-glossary)); Factorio migrations; [I] the exact gate |
| After release | Check Android vitals weekly. Fix the crashes that hit the most players first. Hotfix A bugs at once and batch the rest into a regular patch | [E] 1% of bugs cause half of errors ([Redmond](https://redmondmag.com/articles/2002/10/03/microsoft-error-reporting-drives-bug-fixing-efforts.aspx)); vitals thresholds |

### Severity scale

Severity says how bad the bug is. Priority says when to fix it. Keep them separate [E: Mullich; MIT]. The scale is Bates'/Mullich's A/B/C [E]. Which tycoon bugs go in which class is inferred [I].

| Class | Meaning | Examples in this game | Default priority |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A: Critical** | Crash, freeze, lost or corrupted save, wrong money, or a soft-lock the player can't escape (for example, no cash and no way to earn) | Offline catch-up pays the wrong amount; an old save fails to load; an infinite-money exploit; the app loses progress when killed | Fix now, before any new feature |
| **B: Major** | A feature is broken but the game stays playable or has a workaround. Several B bugs together count as one A | The mill ignores the storage cap; a price tag shows the wrong number; an upgrade button does nothing | Before the next release |
| **C: Minor** | Cosmetic, typo, small annoyance | "100.0" shown instead of "100"; an icon misaligned on a tall phone | Later, or "won't fix (known shippable)" with a one-line reason |

The priority labels are **Now / Next release / Later / Won't fix**. "Known shippable" is a legitimate status. Writing down why a bug is acceptable stops it from being re-triaged every week [E: The Coalition glossary, Vice].

### Bug report template

[E] The fields come from MIT's template, Mullich, Spolsky and the *Industries of Titan* reporter. [I] The save, clock and "bug or design note" fields are added for this game.

```
Title:          (one line — what went wrong, where)
Build / commit: (git commit or version shown in game)
Platform:       PC / Android (phone model)
Severity:       A / B / C        Priority: Now / Next release / Later / Won't fix
Type:           Bug (game does something unintended) or Design note (works, but confusing/boring)
Steps:          1. ... 2. ... 3. ...
Expected:       (what should have happened, with numbers)
Actual:         (what did happen, with exact numbers)
How often:      (x out of y tries)
Time context:   device clock time; time away from the game; clock changed? (yes/no)
Attachments:    copy of user://save.json (and save.bak), screenshot, user://logs/godot.log (PC)
--- filled in after the fix ---
Cause:          (one sentence)
Fix:            (one sentence)
Test added:     (test name / seed)
Verified in game on: (date, platform)
```

## Conclusion

The evidence points away from more manual hunting and toward an exact, repeatable record of what "correct" means, checked by a machine every time. That is how Rare cut its peak bug count by an order of magnitude and how Factorio stopped regressions. It suits your game better than most, because your rules don't depend on graphics, timing or luck. Wrong offline money, a broken old save, a refund exploit or a data typo can each become a test that fails in seconds without anyone playing. Your project has already built most of the hard parts: random play with invariants, the away-versus-playing parity check and data validation. The remaining wins are small and mechanical: commit those scripts, start the old-save library, make the test run automatic with a hook, and raise a few Godot warnings.

The AI evidence changes what your role is. Claude can write tests, run bisect, sweep the code and review changes faster than a person. But the same research shows AI agents edit tests to pass, report flaws that aren't there, and make developers feel faster than they are. Your most valuable contributions are three that no model replaces: describing exactly what you saw, insisting on a red-then-green test before believing a fix, and playing the real game on a real phone before closing a bug. The open question, which no study has yet answered, is how reliably a non-programmer can judge AI-produced evidence. Keeping that evidence to plain pass/fail output and before-and-after numbers is the best available answer.
